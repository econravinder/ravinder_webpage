About Me

I am a doctoral candidate at the Economics and Planning Unit, Indian Statistical Institute, Delhi Centre, advised by Prof. Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay. I work in empirical development economics, studying education, labour markets, and the rural economy in India. My research asks how the expansion of education — from foundational literacy and numeracy in the early grades to college access in adulthood — shapes earnings, employment, and the wider rural economy.

Methodologically, my work combines large-scale Indian administrative and survey data with applied microeconometric methods — instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, and difference-in-differences and event-study designs — alongside machine-learning tools for prediction and cross-survey imputation. My doctoral thesis comprises three chapters on tertiary and foundational education in India. My current project, Tertiary Education Access, Attainment, and Spillovers: Evidence from Rajasthan, India, studies where India's recent college expansion landed and what it changed.

Working Papers

Castelló-Climent, Amparo, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, and Ravinder. "Transforming Rural Economies through Tertiary Education: Evidence from India." WIDER Working Paper No. 2023/89, 2023.

This paper asks whether college-educated individuals who remain in rural India, rather than migrating to cities, contribute to local prosperity. Using village-level data on roughly 427,000 villages across 27 states from the 2011 Socio-Economic Caste Census and Census of India, we estimate the causal effect of tertiary education on rural development, instrumenting the local share of tertiary-educated residents with the historical location of Catholic missions circa 1911. A one-percentage-point increase in a village's share of tertiary-educated individuals raises per capita consumption by about 7.2 percent, robust to using night-light density as an alternative measure of prosperity. The effect operates through two channels: educated residents access productive private-sector employment locally and in nearby towns, and they raise agricultural productivity by improving households' access to information, technical advice, and crop diversification — pointing to a form of structural transformation taking place within rural areas.

Ravinder. "Foundational Skills and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from India." Working Paper, 2025.

This paper estimates the causal returns to foundational skills — basic literacy and numeracy — on adult earnings in India. Because no nationally representative dataset links childhood learning to later labour market outcomes, I train a random forest model on the Indian Human Development Survey, which tests children aged 8–11 alongside rich household characteristics, and predict those scores into the Periodic Labour Force Survey, which records the same characteristics and adult earnings but no test scores. For identification, I exploit a fuzzy regression discontinuity based on the district female-literacy threshold governing eligibility for the District Primary Education Programme. A 0.1 standard-deviation gain in foundational skills raises earnings by roughly 8 percent, with the discontinuity independently confirmed using directly measured ASER learning scores. The returns operate mainly through occupational sorting — stronger skills raise the probability of regular salaried employment and reduce casual wage work — underscoring that the labour market rewards the quality of schooling, not merely its duration.

Ravinder. "College Expansion, Educational Attainment, and Land Fractionalisation: Evidence from Rajasthan, India." Working Paper, 2026.

After 2003, Rajasthan relaxed private-entry norms and underwent one of India's fastest tertiary expansions, with colleges growing from 552 to 3,393 by 2020 — an 11.3 percent annual rate against a 6.2 percent national average. This paper asks where that expansion landed and what governed its placement. A random forest classifier over 43,136 villages predicts proximity to a new college with high accuracy, yet the dominant predictors are physical and administrative geography — distance to the nearest town, night-light intensity, and location — rather than village literacy, caste composition, or educational need. District-level land fractionalisation in 1999 strongly predicts subsequent growth in college access across bandwidths from 5 to 15 km, and a Callaway–Sant'Anna staggered difference-in-differences design, corroborated by border-district event studies, shows a discretely timed rise in access from 2004. The evidence indicates that college access expanded rapidly but along pre-existing spatial and administrative gradients, reinforcing rather than redrawing them. The project further examines the consequences for tertiary attainment and education-motivated migration.
