About Me

I am a doctoral candidate in the Economics and Planning Unit at the Indian Statistical Institute, Delhi Centre. My research interests lie at the intersection of Human Capital, Development Macroeconomics, the Economics of Education, Labour Economics, and the Rural Economy in India.

My doctoral thesis comprises three chapters on tertiary and foundational education in India: a) Transforming Rural Economies through Tertiary Education: Evidence from India. The main question I address is how college-educated individuals who remain in rural India contribute to rural prosperity and, more broadly, to structural transformation. b) Foundational Skills and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from India. The main question I address is: What are the causal returns to foundational skills in terms of adult earnings in India, in a setting where there is no nationally representative longitudinal dataset linking foundational skills to adult earnings? c) College Expansion, Educational Attainment, and Land Fractionalisation: Evidence from Rajasthan, India. The main questions I address are: Where did Rajasthan's rapid college expansion occur, what governed the placement of these colleges, and what are the consequences of this expansion for tertiary education completion rates?

Methodologically, my work combines large-scale Indian administrative and survey data with applied econometric methods—including instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, and staggered difference-in-differences and event-study designs—alongside machine-learning tools for prediction.

Working Papers

Castelló-Climent, Amparo, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, and Ravinder. "Transforming Rural Economies through Tertiary Education: Evidence from India." WIDER Working Paper No. 2023/89, 2023.

This paper examines whether tertiary-educated individuals who live in rural India contribute to local prosperity. Using village-level data, we estimate the causal impact of tertiary education on rural development, exploiting the location of Catholic missions circa 1911, historically associated with the expansion of higher education, as an instrumental variable. A one percentage point increase in the village share of tertiary-educated individuals raises per capita consumption by about 7.2 percent, a result robust to using night-light density as an alternative prosperity measure and to a wide range of further checks. Two mechanisms drive this effect: a conventional channel, in which tertiary-educated individuals access productive private-sector employment locally and in nearby towns, and a novel channel, in which they raise agricultural productivity by improving households' access to information, technical advice, and crop diversification. The findings point to a distinct form of structural transformation occurring within rural areas, alongside that driven by migration to cities.

Ravinder. "Foundational Skills and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from India." Working Paper, 2025.

This paper estimates the causal effect of foundational skills—basic literacy and numeracy—on adult earnings in India. The central challenge is that no nationally representative dataset links childhood learning to later labor market outcomes. We overcome this by training a machine learning model on the Indian Human Development Survey—which assesses the test scores of children aged 8-11 alongside their rich household characteristics—and then predicting these scores in the Periodic Labour Force Survey, which records the same characteristics and adult labor market outcomes but no test scores. The resulting individual-level predicted test scores, aggregated at the district level, correlate strongly with independent learning data from the Annual Status of Education Report (ASER). For identification, we exploit a fuzzy regression discontinuity design based on the district female literacy threshold that governed eligibility for the District Primary Education Programme. Eligibility raised predicted test scores by about 0.17 standard deviations at the cutoff, and a 0.1 standard-deviation increase in test scores raises earnings by about 8 percent, with robust effects across specifications. Re-estimating the first stage with actual ASER scores confirms that the jump in learning at the cutoff is present in directly measured data, not an artefact of prediction. We also estimate returns using Learning-Adjusted Years of Schooling—which avoids conditioning on schooling years as a bad control and accounts for both the duration and the quality of schooling—and find returns of 19 to 22 percent per 0.1 standard deviation. A potential mechanism operates primarily through occupational sorting: stronger skills raise the probability of regular wage/salaried employment and reduce casual wage work. These findings underscore that labor market success in a developing economy rewards the quality of schooling—learning—not merely its duration.

Ravinder. "College Expansion, Educational Attainment, and Land Fractionalisation: Evidence from Rajasthan, India." Working Paper, 2026.

After 2003, Rajasthan relaxed private-entry norms and underwent one of India's fastest tertiary expansions: colleges grew from 552 to 3,393 by 2020, an 11.3 percent compound annual rate against a 6.2 percent national average. This paper asks where that expansion landed and what governed its placement. A random forest classifier over 43,136 villages predicts proximity to a new college with high accuracy, yet the dominant predictors are physical and administrative geography—distance to the nearest town, night-time light intensity, and location—not village literacy, caste composition, or educational need. At the district level, land fractionalization in 1999 strongly predicts subsequent growth in college access; the association holds across bandwidths from 5 to 15 km and strengthens monotonically through 2007. A Callaway-Sant'Anna staggered difference-in-differences design on the village panel shows flat pre-trends and a treatment effect rising steadily to roughly 1.8 colleges by the third period, stable across bandwidths and absent in placebo cohorts. Border-district event studies confirm a discretely timed rise from 2004. Together, the evidence indicates that college access expanded rapidly but along pre-existing spatial and administrative gradients, reinforcing rather than redrawing them.
